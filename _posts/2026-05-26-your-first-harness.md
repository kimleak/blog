So you opened the box. You saw the four parts — instructions, tools, memory, guardrails. And you (very reasonably) asked the question I left hanging: *which one should I actually build first?*

Here's my honest answer: instructions. Every single time.

## Why instructions win

Tools, memory, and guardrails are powerful. They're also expensive — you need code, accounts, sometimes whole servers behind them. Instructions cost nothing. You just… type them.

But cheap doesn't mean weak. Good instructions can turn the same model from "kind of helpful intern" into "specialist who actually gets it." Same brain. New job description. Huge difference in what you get back.

## What a real instruction actually looks like

Most people, the first time they try to "prompt" an AI, write something like:

> Be a good writing assistant.

That's not an instruction. That's a wish. Now compare it to this:

> You're my writing coach. Before answering, always ask one question to clarify what I'm working on. Keep replies under 3 sentences. If a sentence I wrote is boring, say so directly — don't soften it.

Same brain. Wildly different employee.

The trick is to give the AI three things: a **role**, a **style**, and a **constraint**. Role tells it who to be. Style tells it how to talk. Constraint tells it what *not* to do. Most people remember the role and forget the other two — which is why their AI feels generic.

## The fridge magnet rule

Here's how I think about it. Imagine your instructions are a list of fridge magnets your assistant reads every morning before work. The fewer, sharper, more specific magnets — the better the day.

"Be helpful" is a bad magnet. "Always ask for the order number before promising anything" is a great one. It's the difference between a vibe and a rule.

A few magnets worth stealing:

- "Don't write more than 4 lines per paragraph"
- "End every answer with one follow-up question"
- "If you're not sure, say so — don't guess"
- "Never use the words 'leverage' or 'synergy'"

Notice — none of those need any code. They just need *clarity*.

## Try this today (it takes five minutes)

Pick one thing you use AI for. Could be drafting emails, brainstorming gift ideas, summarizing articles — anything you do more than once a week.

Now write five lines of instructions for it. Give it a role, a style, two constraints, and one thing it must never do. Save those five lines somewhere you won't lose them.

Paste them at the top of your next chat. Use the same chunk for a week — not a fresh one each time. The *same one*.

Then watch what happens. You'll start noticing which lines do real work and which ones just sound nice. Edit. Sharpen. Steal lines from your own answers when something works.

## What you're really learning

This isn't a productivity trick. You're learning to *think in harnesses* — to see AI as something you shape, not just something you query.

Most people will spend the next decade reacting to whatever AI feels like doing that day. A small group will spend it shaping AI to do what they want, on purpose, on repeat. The only thing separating those two groups is whether anyone ever bothered to write their own five lines.

So — what are *yours* going to be?
